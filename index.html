How it works

Set it up once. It runs every day.

No app for your parent to install, no account for them to remember, no screen to learn. Their phone rings, and they talk.

1

You add your parent

Their name, their number, a time of day that suits them, and anything worth asking about — the neighbour who visits, the knee that plays up, the grandchild they ask after.

2

Amatra calls them

Sam in English, Priya in Hindi. A few minutes, at the same time each day. Not an interrogation — the same handful of questions a daughter would ask if she had the time.

3

You get the update

What they said, what they need, and what's changed since last week. Waiting for you when you finish work — or on your sister's phone in another city.

The record nobody else has

When something does happen, a doctor gets fifteen minutes and whatever your parent can remember. Amatra keeps a dated account of ordinary days, so the conversation starts from something real.

The whole care circle

Invite your brother, your cousin, the aunt who lives nearby. Everyone sees the same updates, so nobody's relaying news down a chain of phone calls — and the person doing the most stops doing it alone.

Amatra is a record of what your parent said on the phone. It doesn't diagnose anything, doesn't give medical advice, and isn't an emergency service. If something's wrong, call a doctor.

Why we built it

The three of us grew up in Dubai. Every Sunday our parents called their parents in India — the same questions, the same worry, the same long-distance line.

Then we left for Indiana, and it was our parents doing the calling. Checking we'd eaten. Checking we were alright.

Now they're the ones getting older, and we're the ones on the other side of the world with a meeting at nine. Nobody in this story ever stopped caring. There just stopped being time.

Questions people ask

The ones that matter.

Does my parent know it isn't a real person?

Yes. At the start of every call, Sam says she's an AI assistant calling on behalf of the family, and that the call is recorded. Nobody is being tricked — that would be the fastest way to lose their trust, and yours.

Won't they just refuse to talk to it?

Some do at first. What we've found is that people are often more open with someone who isn't family — there's no one to worry, and nothing to admit. A parent who insists they're fine to their daughter will happily tell Sam the hip's been bad since Tuesday.

What if they don't answer?

Amatra doesn't ring and ring, and it doesn't leave a message and pretend that counts. You'll see that the call wasn't answered — and if it happens two days running, that's exactly the kind of thing you want to know about.

Isn't this just outsourcing the call I should be making?

It's the call on the days you can't. Most people we've spoken to aren't trying to call less — they're trying to stop the six-week gaps where nobody noticed anything. When you do call, you'll have something to talk about beyond "how are you, fine".

Is this a medical or emergency service?

No. Amatra records what your parent said in their own words and shows it to your family. It doesn't assess anyone's health, give advice, or respond to emergencies. It's useful to a doctor because it's a dated account of ordinary days — the interval history that otherwise doesn't exist.

Which languages, and which countries?

English and Hindi today, with more to come. We call the US, Canada, India, the UK, Germany and a number of others. The UAE isn't supported yet — we'll tell you honestly rather than take your money and find out.

What does it cost?

Nothing while we're testing. We're looking for a small number of families to use it properly and tell us where it falls short. There'll be a subscription later, and you'll know the price well before you're ever asked for it.

Who can see my parent's calls?

Only the people you invite into your care circle. You add them, and you can remove them. You can delete your account and everything in it at any time.
